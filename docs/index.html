The idea

Three things no other client puts in front of you

Where a post lives

Every account sits on a server, and which one is public information nobody surfaces. Relays reads it out of the DID document and writes it under the post. You can filter by it.

02

Who moderated it

Moderation here is a choice between services, not a fact of nature. Subscribe to the ones you trust, set every label yourself — and on anything hidden or covered, Relays says which layer decided and who applied it.

The network itself

Your server, your name

You are never asked where your account lives

Sign in with a handle. Relays resolves it to the server that actually hosts it — through the PLC directory or the domain itself — and talks to that one. There is no server field to get wrong, and self-hosted accounts work the same as everyone else‘s.

And it tells you the answer before you type a password. Type a handle and the line under the field names the machine that account is on, whether it is up, and how many accounts it holds — all of it public, none of it requiring you to be signed in to anything. Nobody else says it out loud.

Relays signs in with an app password, never your account password. It lives in the Keychain and can be revoked without touching your account. Making a new account is not in the app — see for why.

The readings screen already estimated this from the firehose. A sample tells you who is writing this minute, weighted by how much they write; the register tells you who exists, weighted by how many accounts they hold. Those are different questions, so Relays shows both and labels which is which.

Moderation

Two layers, and you can tell them apart

What the network knows travels with your account and holds in every client: the moderation services you subscribe to, your setting for each of their labels, muted words, mute and block lists, who may reply to your posts and who may quote them.

What only your phone knows never leaves it: local feed rules with regular expressions, domain and handle filters, and a rule for showing only self-hosted accounts — things the protocol has no place to store.

Anything covered says which of the two decided it, and names the service if a service did. A moderation decision with no author is indistinguishable from a bug.

Everything it does

Features

Written against the AT Protocol directly — 66 endpoints, no intermediary service of its own. Where something has a caveat, the caveat is here too.

Reading

  • Your following timeline, saved feeds and lists, in one switcher
  • Threads with the whole chain of parents, and replies shown with who they answer
  • Discovery on the search screen before you type: feeds other people keep, accounts the network suggests
  • Search across accounts and posts, hashtags, and the posts that quote a given post
  • Every counter leads somewhere: who liked a post, who reposted it, who quoted it
  • A quoted feed, list, starter pack or moderation service is drawn as itself — and a quote that is gone says whether it was deleted, blocked, or taken back by its author
  • Yesterday‘s page shows instantly from a local cache while today‘s loads

Posting

  • Up to four pictures with alt text, or one video
  • Links, hashtags and @-mentions resolved as you type — indexed in UTF-8 bytes, the way the protocol counts
  • Quotes, replies, reposts, and deleting your own posts
  • Reply and quote rules per post: everyone, people you follow, people you mention, your followers, or nobody
  • Fold a reply away in a thread you started — hidden for everyone reading it, still there in the network

Moderation

  • Subscribe to moderation services and set each of their labels to show, warn or hide
  • Labels act: posts are hidden, covered, blurred or flagged, with the reason and its source
  • Muted words with targets, expiry, and an exception for people you follow
  • Mute and block lists — subscribe to somebody else‘s, or keep your own
  • Report to a service you chose, not only to whichever one your server picks
  • Hide a single post for yourself; block, mute, and see who blocked you
  • A sensitive-content switch that is off until you turn it on

Messages

  • Conversations, history, and starting a new one from a search
  • Mute a conversation, leave it, or report a single message
  • Set who may message you: everyone, people you follow, or nobody

The relay

  • The hairline under the wordmark carries the live firehose — brighter where the network wrote more
  • Tap the title for throughput, median latency, composition, and a sampled ranking of servers with the share that are self-hosted
  • The relay‘s own register: every server it reads from, with account counts and whether each is up — searchable, and told apart from the sample it sits under
  • The sign-in screen‘s backdrop is the same stream: the last minute of the network, before you have signed in to anything
  • Type a handle there and it names the server that handle lives on, before any password

Notifications

  • All thirteen kinds the protocol defines, each with its own words — and a fourteenth it has not met yet still gets a sentence rather than a raw identifier
  • Settings that live on your account, not on the phone: per kind, off, in the list, or worth interrupting you for — and they apply in every client you use
  • A reply opens the reply, not the post it answered
  • Muted accounts stay muted here too

Provenance

  • The hosting server printed under every post
  • A record inspector: the raw JSON of any post, its DID, CID, AT URI and facets
  • Export your whole repository as a signed CAR file — every post, like and follow, readable offline

The app itself

  • Four grounds — light, dim, dark and Bluesky blue — and two languages
  • Three text sizes, and it follows the system size up to 145%
  • VoiceOver throughout: a post reads as one thing, not as seven
  • Says when you are offline instead of blaming the server, and reloads when the connection returns
  • Several accounts, switched without signing out
  • A real Mac app, not a stretched phone one: ⌘R and a refresh control where a Mac has no pull gesture

Privacy Policy

Last updated 3 September 2026

Relays collects nothing about you. It has no servers of its own, no analytics, no crash reporting and no third-party code. What follows is a description of where your data goes instead — which is to the server you chose, and nowhere else.

Who is responsible

Relays is made by Benjamin Stiller. Questions about this policy go to the address on the page.

What Relays sends, and where

Relays talks to the server that hosts your account and to the services that server works with, and it reads two public things from a relay without signing in: the register of servers on the network, and the live stream of records. Neither carries anything about you. Depending on what you do in the app, what goes to your server includes:

  • Your handle and DID — the public names your account is known by across the network.
  • Your display name, description, profile picture and banner — when you edit your profile.
  • Posts, replies, alt text and attached pictures or video — when you write them. Video is processed by Bluesky‘s video service.
  • Direct messages — when you send them, to Bluesky‘s chat service.
  • Your moderation settings — subscribed services, per-label choices, muted words, saved feeds and reply rules. These live in your account‘s preferences so they work in every client.
  • Your notification settings — which kinds reach you, and from whom. These live on your account too, so they apply in every client, not only this one.

Everything in that list is data you are publishing or storing on your own account. Relays is the thing that carries it there.

What stays on your device

  • Your credential — an app password, held in the iOS Keychain. It never leaves the device except to sign in to your server.
  • Your settings — theme, language, text size and local feed rules, in the app‘s own preferences store.
  • Local feed rules — keywords, regular expressions, domains and handles you filter on. These are deliberately never uploaded.
  • A cache of recent posts, so a feed can appear before the network answers. Deleting the app removes it.

Tracking

There is none. Relays contains no analytics SDK, no advertising identifier, no crash reporter and no third-party libraries. Nothing about your use of the app is measured, and no profile of you is built anywhere.

The network is public

The AT Protocol is an open network. Posts, likes, follows, blocks and profile information are public records that anyone can read, including people not using Bluesky at all. Relays does not change that, and cannot. Direct messages are not public, but they are stored by the chat service, not encrypted end-to-end.

Children

The network requires you to be at least 13, and that is checked where accounts are made — which is not in this app. Relays signs in to an account that already exists.

Deleting your account

You can delete your account from inside the app, under Settings → Delete account. Your server sends a code by email, and the deletion removes everything the account holds. It cannot be undone.

Changes

If this policy changes, the date above changes with it.

Things that are answered here already

"This app password cannot use direct messages." Ordinary app passwords cannot read messages — the server will not issue a token for them. Create one with message access in your account settings and sign in with that.

"Your sign-in no longer works." The app password was revoked or expired. Relays signs you out rather than retrying something that cannot succeed; sign in again with a current one.

Notifications arrive late. Relays checks in the background and delivers what it finds. How often iOS gives it a turn is up to iOS, and there is no push service behind the app to hurry it along.

Turning a notification kind off changed my other app too. That is correct, and it is the point. Those settings live on your account rather than on this phone, so every Bluesky client you use reads the same ones. Settings → What reaches you.

I cannot create an account. You cannot, not from here. Bluesky‘s server advertises that it needs a phone number and then declines to send a code, so the form was taken out rather than left in a state nobody could finish. Make the account in another client or on the web, then sign in here with an app password.

A feed is empty after signing in. Saved feeds come from your account‘s preferences. If the connection dropped while they were loading, pull to refresh or reopen the app.

Sensitive content will not turn on. It is a setting on your account, and it is off by default. Settings → Moderation → Sensitive content.

Reporting content

Every post, account and message can be reported from its menu inside the app. Reports go to a moderation service, which acts on them — Relays cannot remove anything from the network itself. Blocking and muting take effect immediately and are yours alone.

Deleting your account

Settings → Delete account. Your server emails you a code; the deletion needs that code and your password. It is permanent and it removes everything the account holds.
